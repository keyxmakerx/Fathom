The figure is small on purpose

A computed node-link figure coupled to a dense register, locked in both directions. The picture carries shape; the register carries the set. 52 §3.6.1 already says the honest answer above the element ceiling is the table — this builds that answer rather than apologising for it.

Fathom never connects to anything. Nothing here is polled, probed or monitored — every fact below is parsed, entered or derived.

Fixture. Thesis

what changed, and what it cost

In one sentence

Make the picture small and exact, give the lines a real vocabulary, and put everything the picture cannot hold into a dense register locked to it — a monochrome technical drawing looks handsome when it is drafted, and the five rejected concepts were not drafted, they were typed.

1. Layout is computed. All of it.

There is not one hard-coded node coordinate in this file. A rank pass, a width pass, a port-lattice pass, an orthogonal router with a route table, an interval-graph channel allocator with a centring pass, a crossing pass and a label placer run on every change. Move the estate control and watch every number move. The five rejected concepts had five arrays of literal coordinates, which is why none of them had rhythm — nothing made them land on anything.

2. Two stroke weights, exactly 2:1

Device and external-peer boundaries at 2px; every edge, band, bracket, stub, tick, chassis rule and arrowhead at 1px. That is ISO 128's governing proportion and it is the single largest visual change from the rejected round, where a device boundary, a link and a zone bracket were literally the same mark. 1px against 1.5px reads as an antialiasing artefact; 1px against 2px reads as two weights.

3. Three neutral fills, and fill means what a thing is

Canvas --surface-2, region wash --surface, device box --page. The rejected concepts spent the one completely unspent channel — fill — on hover, and gave structure nothing. Fill here never encodes a pointer state. Selection is ▸, a --surface ground and 40% opacity on everything outside the closure, which is what 56 §6.1 already specified.

4. Nothing touches anything

Every edge leaves an 8px perpendicular stub outside the box, on a 4px lattice along the box edge. No edge lands flush on a wall and no edge crosses a device box — verified, 0 violations at every estate size. Zabbix's centre-to-centre line is the whole mechanical reason its maps look like 2004, and four of the five rejected concepts drew exactly that line. (The research spec also asked for a 4px gap between the stub end and the rail; I refused it, and the reason is in What I sacrificed.)

5. Sides are semantic

Uplinks leave the top, downlinks the bottom, management the left, tunnels the right — regardless of chassis slot. It costs no channel and it is a bigger legibility win than any stroke change: you can read the direction of the estate without reading a single label.

6. The device box became a rack elevation

This is the change that fixed the picture, and it was not on anyone's list. A hub terminating twelve tunnels drew a 400px-tall empty rectangle with twelve stubs hanging off one edge — the box was a container with nothing in it. It is now a header row, two chassis sub-rows, and one identical 32px unit row per st0 unit, with that unit's conduit leaving from its own row. The peer opposite it sits on the same centre line, so every conduit is a zero-bend straight run: aesthetic rule 12's best case is not a cheap route, it is no route at all. Repeated identical modules on a common baseline is where rack elevations, ISO part drawings and PCB silkscreen get their looks, and it is the posture — the dense matrix — applied to the node itself.

7. Two numbers I changed, and why

56 §5.3 sets --dg-rail-gap: 3px and --dg-conduit-gap: 5px in scene units while every stroke carries non-scaling-stroke and stays 1 CSS px. At zoom 0.5 that is 1.5px of white inside a conduit; at the 0.35 LOD floor it is 1.75px total, and both the LAG twin-rail and the tunnel degenerate into the single undifferentiated line the owner complained about — at exactly the zoom where you are looking at a whole site. Both are 8px here, which puts each rail on the 4px lattice at ±4. Making them equal costs nothing, because G4's distinction was never the gap — it is 2 separate versus 2 capped, and the caps carry it. Below zoom 0.35 the conduit collapses to a single capped 2px rail and the view band says conduit collapsed rather than letting it merge silently.

8. Crossings break, junctions get a square, and break count is a constraint

A four-way meeting with nothing drawn at it means CONNECTED in every schematic convention there is, which is the opposite of what a crossing means. Every genuine crossing is a 4px gap in the lower-priority rail; every genuine T is a 4px filled square.

Two deviations, both deliberate. The break is a gap in the rail's own path data, not a --page knockout drawn over it: a knockout dies under forced-colors, and it cannot be painted in one layer without also erasing whatever is on top of it. A gap costs zero elements and survives export, print and high contrast. And break count is capped: a rail that already carries three breaks stops taking them and the other rail breaks instead. A rail broken a dozen times reads as a dashed rail, and dash is reserved — so this is a constraint, not a cost term. The same reasoning moved the two band-anchored edges (the static route, the zone-pair policy set) onto a perimeter lane: driving them through the conduit fan would have been shorter and would have broken them twelve times each.

9. Labels are decided in groups, never one at a time

Half a row labelled and half silently dropped is the exact texture that reads as generated. Three group rules, all announced in the view band:

  • A whole side is labelled or a whole side is folded. The decision comes from the measured stub pitch against the widest label on that side. A side that will not fit on one row is staggered over two, which is PCB silkscreen's own answer.
  • Identical labels are folded. Six rails carrying vlan 10 in one corridor do not need the word six times; a drawing says it once. Same for a repeated port name across a rank.
  • The label carries what differs. Every tunnel's selector begins 10.1.0.0/16, so the picture prints only the remote half and the register prints both.

At 12 spokes that is 1 label hidden and 18 folded, against 18 hidden and 19 moved before the rules existed.

10. The corridor is sized by what it has to carry

redraw() runs the layout twice: pass one measures the channel count in every corridor from the interval-graph colouring, pass two places the ranks with corridors tall enough for those channels plus the two staggered port-label rows at each end. A fixed corridor is what makes a generated picture cramped in one place and empty in another. Two passes cost about 1 ms.

What I sacrificed

  • Estate width. The figure is deliberately capped. The hub's unit column holds 12 tunnels before the box passes the 480px legibility cap — a number computed from the 32px module, not chosen — and above it the remainder aggregates into one external-peer box labelled +29 PEERS, announced as endpoints aggregated. Every aggregated tunnel still has an exact row in the register. Turn aggregate off and the figure draws all 40: it stays correct, on the lattice and legible, and stops being small — the hub becomes 1,372px tall and the scene 2,124px. That is the trade this study exists to make visible.
  • §4.2's chassis sub-rows for the reth. The members leave the top edge because the top edge is the uplink side, and the sub-rows are horizontal, so two members cannot sit at two different sub-row heights on that edge. The bracket at the device end and the redundancy group in the label are both drawn; which chassis owns which member is in the register and the inspector. Named, not hidden.
  • The 4px gap between a stub end and its rail. Refused. With a gap meaning "these two rails cross without connecting" everywhere else in this picture, a gap at a terminal would say the cable is not plugged in. The stub is 8px of stand-off from the box wall — nothing lands flush — and the conductor is continuous from there. A policy-set edge does stand off by 8px with no stub at all, because it is not a cable, and that contrast is the point.
  • The zone VPN bracket. Degraded to 56 §4.4's own tier 3 — a 4px tick on each member's row and the name once — because a vertical bracket there would have crossed all twelve conduits, and a stroke crossed twelve times says nothing reliable about what it encloses. Tier 3 then forces §4.4's other rule: the policy-set edge runs to the device that owns the zone and the label carries both zone names, TRUST → VPN · 4 · deny. Announced as security · 1 zone shown as ticks.
  • Node names. Widths quantise to 64/96/128/160/192 and names end-truncate with an ellipsis. A column of identical boxes reads as drawn; a ragged one reads as generated. Boxes also widen to hold their own ports and their own age line, so width is a computed consequence, not a free variable.
  • Hatch. Deferred. The three tonal fills may well be enough, and 51 §4.1 C2 already spends hatch twice at 135°.

Measured, in Chromium, at DPR 1

  • Zero console errors. Zero off-file requests. The CSP declares connect-src 'none' and form-action 'none'; the only request the page makes is ../tokens.css over file://.
  • 4 / 12 / 40 spokes: 13/22/25 nodes, 20/30/36 drawn edges, 135/213/222 live SVG elements, layout 0.7/0.8/1.4 ms and render 0.5/1.3/0.8 ms (median of five). Element ceiling is 2,000.
  • 40 spokes, aggregation off: 53 nodes, 64 edges, 475 elements, 2.3 ms layout — a 1,472 × 2,124 scene. It survives; it stops being small.
  • No edge overlaps any node box: 0 violations at every size. Every stub is exactly 8px. Every node position, node size and route vertex is a multiple of 4: 0 off-lattice values.
  • Bijection: 52 drawn elements, 52 register rows, 0 drawn elements without a row (56 §7).
  • Keyboard: all 52 rows reachable by arrow key from one tab stop; all 22 drawn nodes show the 2px mirrored ring in the picture; 69 of 70 frames carry a 2px outline on the focused row.
  • The chip meets 1.4.13 on all three counts — shown by :focus as well as hover, dismissed by Escape without moving the pointer, survives the pointer entering it, still there after 1,800 ms. box-shadow: none, 1px --ink border, and the source contains no setTimeout at all.
  • The half-pixel question, answered. With crisp on, a 1px rail's geometric edge lands at fraction 0.500 of a device pixel, so the stroke covers exactly one device row. With it off it lands at 0.000 and the stroke straddles two rows at partial alpha — which is what makes a whole picture of hairlines look washed out and is very hard to name. Toggle it and look. Two-line fix, and it was worth the ten minutes.

On colour: I did not reopen it, and I do not think it is the problem

Six of Bertin's seven retinal variables survive monochrome; only hue is lost. The rejected concepts left three of the remaining six completely unused — value (they filled --page boxes on a --page ground), size (one box height, one stroke weight) and texture. Spending ADR-0011's three risk colours here would buy one channel they already had five substitutes for, and would cost the only unambiguous safety signal the product has — on the most-circulated artifact the product makes. Look at the inspector: the risk colours appear there, on command lines, where they mean what a command does to a live box. That is the whole argument, drawn.

Which disclosure I would ship

Ship the register column as the mechanism. Ship the static ▸ mark before either. Ship the chip as an optional preview, bound by a content rule.

Both are built here — flip Disclosure and feel the difference. The chip satisfies WCAG 1.4.13 in full: dismissible with Escape without moving the pointer, hoverable (no gap, a grace corridor, pointer-events on), persistent with no timer at all, and it is triggered by :focus as well as :hover, so it is not hover-only. It carries no shadow, because 51 §11 forbids elevation — a 1px --ink rule is enough, which is also what containerlab's label chips and NetBox's halo independently concluded.

And it is still the wrong mechanism. Turn it on and read what it says: usually one line, ↵ inspector. That is the content rule working — the chip may contain only what the picture already tried to draw and could not. Under that rule it is a label that would not fit, and the moment you let it hold anything else you have built an inspector that is unreachable by keyboard, invisible to the SVG export, absent from the print, and occluding exactly the neighbours the user is comparing against, at the density where they needed the picture most.

The owner's real question is not "can I hover" — it is "is there more here?", and a hover cannot answer that, because you must already be pointing at the thing to find out. The ▸ at a node's bottom-right answers it statically, at every zoom, in print, on touch, in the exported SVG and for a screen-reader user, and it costs no new glyph and no new interaction model: ▸ already means there is more here in the config gutter, the finder, the view rail and the inspector. Two of the rejected concepts spent it on "selected", which collides with all four.

What did not work

(generated at render time)

  • The reth's chassis rows. I could not draw §4.2's picture and keep semantic sides at the same time, and I chose semantic sides. If the owner would rather see the two members leave two different chassis rows, the reth has to move to the left or right edge and the WAN uplink stops being the top edge — which costs more than it buys.
  • Crossing density in the access fan. Phases 3–6 of 56 §3.3 (cycle break, dummies, crossing reduction, Brandes-Köpf) are not built — §3.4 scopes them as later work — so the only crossing reduction here is the barycentric stub ordering. At six access switches a few rails still take four or five breaks. The break budget keeps that from becoming a dash, but a real crossing-minimisation sweep would remove most of them outright.
  • Band strokes still pass under edges without a break. Bands are not in the crossing set; the tone step (--muted against --ink) is doing that work alone. It reads, but it is a weaker signal than a break and it is the reason the zone VPN bracket had to degrade to ticks rather than be drawn across the conduit fan.
  • The perimeter lanes are a compromise. Routing the static route and the policy set around the outside keeps them unbroken, but it makes two long rails that carry no information along most of their length. A layout that put the routing-instance box and the zone brackets nearer their targets would not need them.
