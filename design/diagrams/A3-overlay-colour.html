In one sentence: A-schematic was right about everything except scale and colour, so this build adds exactly two tiers on top of it and changes nothing else — like-kind sibling groups collapse at six into a stub that says what it is hiding and how many, and the picture stays monochrome until you pick an overlay, at which point the tint arrives together with a key that the choice generated and with a drawn word on every element the tint touches.

The finding that amends the corpus

44 §4.7.4's 2,000-element ceiling is a PERFORMANCE ceiling and the corpus has never specified the LEGIBILITY one. That is the finding, and it is the reason this build exists. §4.7.4 decides "the diagram never renders more than 2,000 live SVG elements. Above that it aggregates"; 52 §3.6.1 repeats it as an IA consequence; 56 §12 carries the unit-level case as an open decision — "a hub with 40 spokes has st0.0 through st0.39, and drawing 40 units on one device box is a texture… needs a design pass and a hub fixture."

Measured here: the 40-spoke un-aggregated render is 515 SVG elements. That is a quarter of the way to §4.7.4's ceiling and it is already unreadable — forty identical interface labels down a 1,332px device column, forty parallel rails, forty identical boxes, and the genuinely interesting content (two zones, a VLAN, the cluster pair, the access layer, the routing instance) squashed into the lower-left corner at fit 0.68. The element budget is not the thing that bites. The legibility ceiling fires two orders of magnitude earlier, it fires on LIKE-KIND SIBLING COUNT rather than on total element count, and 44 §4.7.4 should be amended to say so. This page is the design pass 56 §12 asks for, and the hub fixture it asks for is the estate above.

The threshold, and why it is six

Six, and it is derived rather than chosen: --pitch ÷ --portPitch = 96 ÷ 16 = 6. A like-kind sibling group of more than six units is, by construction, taller than the routing channel that separates two whole ranks of the estate — the exact count at which a repeated group stops being a feature of one node and becomes the dominant vertical dimension of the picture. Both numbers are already tokens, so the threshold moves if the lattice moves and it cannot drift on its own.

Two independent lines land on the same number, which is the reason to trust it. Perceptually, one to four items are apprehended at a glance, five and six by grouping, and beyond that a human counts serially — at which point a printed count is strictly better than the thing being counted, because +40 is read in one fixation and forty boxes are not. Historically, schematic capture has used a bus with a slash and a count rather than n parallel wires since the 1970s, at roughly the same crossover. And round one's concept 03 chose eight with no derivation and the corpus never contradicted it, so six is inside the range the corpus tolerates and is the conservative end of it.

The threshold is a control, not a constant — at 4 / at 6 / at 8 above the picture. Move it and watch where it starts to cost you: at four the reth pair and the two-member LAG begin to disappear, which is exactly the "collapsed something I wanted to see" failure; at eight the hub is 172px tall before anything happens.

What aggregation does, at three levels

  • Unit level. st0.0…st0.39 becomes one stub inside the hub reading st0.0–st0.39 · +40. This is also the port-level case: units and ports are the same n.sides[side] group and share one code path.
  • Peer level. SPOKE-01…SPOKE-40 becomes one box reading 40 spokes over SPOKE-01–SPOKE-40, carrying G13 — the stack mark, a second 1px --muted outline offset one --s1 behind the boundary. Geometry, not colour; it survives greyscale, print and forced-colours.
  • Member level. ae0's member interfaces collapse to one residual stub reading xe-0/0/2–xe-0/0/17 · +16. G5's bracket-at-both-ends survives, because a second unnamed stub is kept so the bracket still has a spine: aggregation does not get to spend an edge-vocabulary channel.
  • The two ends of the tunnel fan collapse together, deliberately. Collapsing the units without the peers would land forty rails on one stub, which is worse than the texture it replaced.

Expansion is a ladder, not a cliff — and why partial

Partial, and this is the decision I would defend hardest. One activation reveals threshold − 1 = five members and leaves the rest collapsed in a residual stub that is itself a tab stop. Two consequences fall out and neither is a chosen number: an expanded group never exceeds the threshold that collapsed it, and a residual is never left below the threshold — a group that small would never have been collapsed in the first place, so the last step jumps straight to full. Measured ladder at forty: 0 → 5 → 10 → 15 → 20 → 25 → 30 → 40 → 0. Full expansion in one keystroke would re-create the exact texture the mechanism exists to remove; making you walk down a ladder is the point.

Esc collapses from anywhere; a second activation collapses once the group is fully expanded, at which point the stub is replaced by a ▾ 40 tunnels shown · collapse chip so there is always something to activate. collapse all is in the control band.

Nothing is ever silently dropped

The count is stated in five places at once, and the mechanism is built so that it cannot lose: the stub's own label, the aggregate box's two lines, the conduit's +35 tunnels midpoint label, the affordance's accessible name, the SVG root's accessible name, the Outline row, and G10's view band — diagram · 10 nodes · 15 edges · 40 tunnels collapsed · 16 ae0 members collapsed, which is precisely what 56 §5.2's release valve is for. An aggregate's count reserves its space in the label grid before any other label competes for it, so a count can never be the label that loses a collision; and the conduit-label suppression that hides forty midpoint selectors is explicitly bypassed for an aggregate, because there the label is the count.

The overlay tier — and why it beats always-on colour

The research points at Arista CloudVision: the base drawing is neutral, the user selects an overlay, and "when you choose an overlay, a color key will appear in the display." The key is generated by the choice and absent from the base. Fathom has independently arrived at the same architecture — a monochrome base plus a layer mask — and simply had no overlay tier. This builds it. Five overlays are real and switchable: zone, layer, verification, routing instance, VLAN.

Three properties, and I checked each rather than assuming it. (i) The base stays print- and greyscale-safe — measured, with overlay none the SVG resolves to exactly five painted values and all five are neutral tokens: --ink, --muted, --hairline, --surface, --page. Zero elements carry a tint. (ii) Colour always arrives with a legend — the key is built from the same classify function that painted the picture, so it cannot fall out of step with it, and it is destroyed when you deselect. (iii) Nobody inherits a map whose colours mean whatever the last engineer chose — which is Apstra's failure, where colour is user-assigned per node and a colleague opening the map has no key at all. Here colour is a pure function of the graph: the class words are sorted and mapped to --ov-1…5 in order, so the same estate produces the same colours in every session, on every machine, for every engineer. There is no node.color, which is also what 56's governing rule demands.

The rule I did not break, and the one invariant that enforces it

THE INVARIANT: nothing is tinted that is not also tagged. Every element the overlay tints also carries the class word, drawn, in the picture. If the word will not fit, the element keeps its neutral stroke and the refusal is counted in the view band as n untinted — no room for the word. It is enforced in render() and asserted in the harness at every size and every overlay: tinted === tagged, always.

That is the answer to the two failures the research found. Meraki marks a device-in-another-network with a blue diamond — the same shape as its unknown-device diamond — so the distinction dies in greyscale with nothing left behind. netlab draws iBGP, local-AS iBGP, eBGP and confederation eBGP all at stroke-width 4 with identical arrowheads, separated by hue alone. Neither is reproducible here, because in both cases the tint would simply not have been applied.

The node tag sits in the --s3 foot channel A-schematic already reserves for ▸, left-anchored where the mark is right-anchored. No box grows, so switching overlays never reflows the layout — the mental map is untouched, which is the thing 56 §3.5's whole ordering-seed machinery exists to protect.

Greyscale, measured — every distinction and what carries it

Rendered at filter: grayscale(1). The five overlay hues collapse to #535353 #505050 #484848 #4b4b4b #4e4e4e — a 1.20:1 spread, effectively one grey. That is deliberate and it is the correct choice, not an oversight: G1 already spends tone on freshness (--ink vs --muted), and an overlay palette with a lightness ladder would compete with the channel that says whether you can trust the box. Hue carries the class; tone stays G1's; and the word carries the meaning. Distinction by distinction:

  • zone TRUST / WAN / VPN → the zone name drawn inside every tinted box, plus G7's vertical bracket the box already sits in, plus the band label zone WAN.
  • layer physical / L2 / L3 / security / overlay → a three-character tag on every tinted rail, plus 56 §5.4's edge vocabulary, which already separates these losslessly in form: a conduit is two capped rails, a trunk carries two mid-ticks, a route carries an open V, a policy has bracket returns.
  • verification fresh / ageing / stale / unverified → the state word inside the box, plus G8's age line (parsed 11 months ago), plus the rule under an unverified box. Note honestly that this is the one overlay that overwrites an existing channel: tinting the boundary spends G1's tone. The word and the age line are what make that safe, and they are why the invariant is absolute rather than a guideline.
  • routing instance CUST-A / inet.0 → the instance name inside the box, plus G7's closed box around the non-default instance.
  • VLAN → the VLAN set is the edge's own label, and G6's mid-ticks already separate trunk from access.
  • absence — an element no overlay classifies keeps its neutral stroke, has no ribbon and no word, and is counted as its own row in the generated key. A mark whose meaning cannot be looked up is the Meraki defect; so is a legend that omits the most common value.

Does it actually look better, or just busier? — honestly

Aggregation: unambiguously better, and it is not close. At forty spokes the picture goes from 515 elements in a 1000 × 1332 scene at fit 0.68 to 171 elements in an 804 × 620 scene at fit 0.96. The estate that was squashed into the lower-left corner is now the whole drawing, at nearly 1:1, with its zones and its VLAN band and its cluster pair legible. And the picture is now the same size at 12, 40 and 120 spokes — 171 elements, 620px tall, every time. That is the property worth having: the drawing stops being a function of how many identical things you own.

Colour: better for exactly one job, and busier for every other. I will not pretend otherwise. Turning on the zone overlay adds, at twelve spokes, 8 ribbons and 8 words to a picture that had none — and if you were reading the tunnel path, all of it is noise. What it buys is a question the monochrome picture answers slowly and this one answers instantly: is anything in the wrong zone? Three seconds of scanning becomes one glance. That is why it is an overlay and not a palette. Colour that is on all the time is busier all the time; colour that arrives when you ask a question, and leaves with the question, is only busy while you are asking. The default is none and the page opens there.

The one I would ship first is the verification overlay, and not because it is the prettiest — because it is the only one that is fully redundant with an existing channel, so it can be wrong about nothing. The one I am least sure about is VLAN, for a structural reason worth stating: VLAN membership is a set, not a partition, so colour can only ever colour the whole set. A per-VLAN colouring is not expressible, and no palette fixes that. It is a property of VLANs.

What it costs, and what I would not claim

  • G13 is a NEW channel and 56 §5.2's table says "nothing may be added to it without taking something away." I did not take anything away, so this is a proposal, not a fait accompli, and it must be ratified or refused. The honest defence: G13 does not encode a property of the network at all — it encodes a property of the rendering, in the same register as the crossing knockout and the junction square, neither of which is in the table either.
  • Aggregation loses the fan. Forty rails leaving one wall is a texture, but it is also the only view in which you can see that every spoke lands on the same interface. The collapsed form asserts that in words rather than showing it. If the owner's real question is "are they all on reth0.0?", the aggregate answers it and the fan demonstrated it, and those are not the same thing.
  • The threshold is uniform across levels. Six units, six peers, six members, six anything. There is a real argument that peers should collapse later than ports — a peer is a whole object and a port is a detail. I have no evidence for a second number and inventing one would be worse than the uniform rule.
  • 2 of 6 VLAN-bearing elements are left untinted at every size, because the tag will not fit on the reth rail bundle where four labels already compete. That is the invariant working as designed and it is counted in the view band — but it does mean the VLAN overlay is visibly incomplete, and the fix is a fifth candidate position for the tag rather than a relaxation of the rule.
  • The edge overlay tag competes for space with the edge label. It is placed at the 0.35 point so it does not fight the 0.5 label, and it goes through the same greedy candidate machinery, so at high density it starts to lose — which un-aggregates into a genuinely useful signal: if the overlay cannot label it, the picture is too dense and you should collapse something.
  • Everything A-schematic could not do, it still cannot do. x-assignment is the priority method rather than Brandes–Köpf; multi-rank edges run in a margin lane rather than through dummy nodes; the sub-pixel stroke offset is exact only at z = 1 and z = 2; 8 port labels at 4 spokes and 13 at 40-with-16-LAG-members still cannot be placed and are counted rather than overlapped.
  • Not verified. Real assistive-technology output — the accessible names are read out of the DOM, not out of NVDA or VoiceOver, and an SVG <g role="button"> is a construct whose support I can assert for Chromium and not for the rest. Actual print output on paper. Real CVD simulation beyond greyscale (55 §3.2's Viénot transform is not run here). And the font stack: 51 §14's five WOFF2 faces are not inlined yet, so every width in this file is computed from DejaVu Sans Mono metrics that may not be the ones rendering.
